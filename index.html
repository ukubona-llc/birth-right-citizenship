Trump v. Barbara
Nos. 25-364 & 25-365 · 609 U.S. ___ (2026) · decided June 30, 2026

The Court held, 6–3 in the judgment, that children born on U.S. soil to parents who are undocumented or present on temporary status are "subject to the jurisdiction" of the United States within the meaning of the Fourteenth Amendment, and are citizens at birth. Chief Justice Roberts wrote for the Court, anchoring the holding in United States v. Wong Kim Ark (1898) and the common-law rule of birthright citizenship. The ruling struck down the executive order President Trump issued on January 20, 2025, his first day in office — an order no lower court had allowed to take effect.

Justice Jackson filed a twenty-page concurrence, joined by Justice Sotomayor as to its introduction and Part I, directed substantially at Justice Thomas's dissent. Justice Kavanaugh concurred in the judgment on narrower statutory grounds and dissented in part from the constitutional holding. Justice Thomas dissented, joined by Justice Gorsuch; Justices Alito and Gorsuch each also filed separate dissents of their own.

Two Jacobians, checked against y_obs
additional viewpoint · grounded in the slip opinion

The roundtable converged on one story: a race-specific draft, withdrawn, replaced by universal language, enacted — Thomas allegedly differentiating around the discarded version. That story is real; it's in Jackson's concurrence, told through the Trumbull episode the screenshots capture. But having read the actual opinions, it isn't quite what separates Thomas from the six-justice majority.

Two Jacobians, not one

Thomas's dissent doesn't argue the Citizenship Clause is race-limited — he reads it as applying "regardless of race," joined by Gorsuch. His limiting principle is domicile: "subject to the jurisdiction thereof" means complete, permanent allegiance, not mere presence. Jackson's sharpest charge isn't that Thomas smuggled race back into a colorblind text — it's that a domicile test, applied to modern immigration status, reproduces the exclusionary shape of Dred Scott's logic while disclaiming its premise. Two different Jacobians are in play: one about how broad the enacted class is (the Trumbull episode answers this), and one about what "jurisdiction" itself denotes (domicile answers it differently than territorial presence does). The withdrawn-draft history is real evidence for the first dispute. It isn't dispositive of the second.

Extending G = J⊤WJ

Ukubona's own equation from the SVG(J) reference — the metric is sovereign, W is not a free parameter, it's the weighting of what counts as evidence — applies here almost literally. Thomas's dissent draws its evidentiary weight from domicile treatises and private-international-law doctrine on allegiance. The majority draws its weight from Wong Kim Ark's common-law framing and the sweep of Trumbull's own later floor statements, including his own admission that even the infant child of a foreigner born in this land is a citizen under his bill. Both readings are internally coherent given their own W. The unresolved question sits one level up: which body of 1868 evidence deserves the heavier weight. That isn't a Jacobian computation with a unique answer — it's the actual site of the jurisprudential disagreement, and reasonable textualists have argued it for decades before this case existed.

The double-edged geodesic

Worth sitting with, for balance: Wong Kim Ark himself was born to parents lawfully domiciled in San Francisco — not to parents unlawfully present or on a temporary visa. The majority in Barbara holds that fact was incidental to the 1898 Court's reasoning, not load-bearing; Thomas's dissent holds the opposite. Which means the "false geodesic" charge — mistaking a chosen coordinate chart for the manifold — cuts both ways. It's exactly as fair to ask whether a 128-year-old opinion is being stretched past what its own facts decided as it is to ask whether a domicile theory imports a limit the enacted text never stated. The critique the roundtable built is sound. It just isn't a one-way weapon.

Internal consistency vs. external critique

Jackson's move against Thomas is different in kind from the Jacobian-vs-manifold critique above. It isn't "your method forgot it's an approximation" — it's "your own stated commitments, run consistently, don't obviously get you here," pointing at Thomas's long record of arguing for a colorblind Constitution elsewhere. Thomas's likely answer: domicile is not a racial category, so a domicile test applied regardless of race carries no formal tension with colorblindness — at most a disparate-impact concern, which is a different and weaker charge than the one Jackson's rhetoric implies. Both moves are legitimate forms of argument. Neither is a knockout.

None of this resolves which W belongs in the Constitution. It just relocates the disagreement to where it actually lives — and confirms the roundtable's own instinct that the interesting question was never really about birthright citizenship.
